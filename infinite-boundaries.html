Finite Dirt, Infinite Coast: The Paradox of the Enclosed Universe

NoteGroup Information

Group: Hmmmm
Authors: Loo Weng Yan, Lee Xin Ning

Hmmmm… Where is the group photo?

As math majors, we get used to infinity popping up in calculus problems, usually neatly tamed by a friendly limit. But every now and then, infinity breaks out of its algebraic cage and destroys our physical intuition.

Today, we’re looking at two classic historical mind-benders where smooth calculus met geometric reality and caused a massive existential crisis for mathematicians: Gabriel’s Horn and the Coastline Paradox.

Topic 1: Gabriel’s Horn (The Infinite Paint Paradox)

In 1641, long before Newton and Leibniz formalized calculus to torment college students, an Italian physicist and mathematician named Evangelista Torricelli discovered something that broke the minds of 17th-century philosophers. He found an object with a finite volume but an infinite surface area.

They called it Gabriel’s Horn (or Torricelli’s Trumpet).

The Recipe

Take the innocent looking function \(y = \frac{1}{x}\), bound it from \(x = 1\) to \(x = \infty\), and spin it around the \(x\)-axis.

The Proof (Why it breaks your brain)

1. The Volume (\(V\))

Using the disk method, the volume of a solid of revolution is given by the integral of \(\pi [f(x)]^2\) from 1 to \(\infty\):

\[V = \pi \int_{1}^{\infty} [f(x)]^2 \, dx\]

Plugging in \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x}\):

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Volume} &= \pi \int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{x^2} \, dx \\ &= \pi \lim_{t \to \infty} \left[ -\frac{1}{x} \right]_{1}^{t} \\ &= \pi \lim_{t \to \infty} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{t} \right) \\ &= \pi \end{aligned}\]

Look at that. The volume converges beautifully to exactly \(\pi\) cubic units. Hence, the volume is finite.

2. The Surface Area (\(A\))

Now let’s compute the outer surface area using the surface of revolution formula, which integrates \(2\pi f(x) \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx\):

\[A = 2\pi \int_{1}^{\infty} f(x) \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx\]

Since the derivative \(f'(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2}\), our integrand becomes:

\[\text{Surface Area} = 2\pi \int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{x} \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{x^4}} \, dx\]

This looks messy to integrate directly, but we can cheat a little and use a comparison test. Notice that for all \(x \ge 1\), the square root term is strictly greater than 1:

\[\sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{x^4}} > 1\]

Therefore, we can establish our inequality:

\[\text{Surface Area} > 2\pi \int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{x} \, dx\]

We know that the classic harmonic integral \(\int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{x} \, dx\) diverges to infinity. By the Comparison Test, the surface area must be infinite.

The Painter’s Paradox

Think about what this means physically. You could fill the inside of the horn completely with \(\pi\) gallons of paint. However, if you tried to paint the outside surface, you would run out of paint, because the surface area is completely infinite. How can a finite volume bound an infinite surface? Welcome to the ultimate geometric glitch.

Bridging to the Coastline Paradox

We paired Gabriel’s Horn with the Coastline Paradox because they are structural twins. The horn shows a finite volume trapped by an infinite surface area. The Coastline Paradox shows a finite landmass area trapped by an infinite perimeter.

Topic 2: The Coastline Paradox (When limits don’t behave)

If someone asks you, “How long is the coastline of Great Britain?”, you’d probably look it up on Wikipedia like a normal person, write down the number, and move on.

But in 1967, mathematician Benoît Mandelbrot proved that doing this makes you fundamentally wrong. His shocking conclusion? The answer depends entirely on how long your ruler is.

If you measure the island using a giant 100km ruler from space, you skip right over tiny bays, beaches, and jagged rocks, giving you a short, neat measurement. If you walk the coast with a standard 30cm ruler, you suddenly have to trace around every single large boulder, small inlet, and curve, making the total distance skyrocket.

If you decide to be an overachiever and measure around every grain of sand with an atomic-sized ruler, the perimeter stretches out toward infinity.

To see how a shape can have an infinite perimeter but a finite area, we use a perfect mathematical model called the Koch Snowflake.

The Calculus Behind It:

Step 1: Introducing the Koch Snowflake

Imagine starting with a simple, flat line segment that is exactly 3 units long:

[———1 unit———][———1 unit———][———1 unit———] (Total: 3)

So, \(P_0 = 3\)

Now, we apply a repeating rule to this line:

  1. Divide the line into 3 equal pieces.

  2. Erase the middle piece.

  3. Replace that missing middle piece with an outward triangle point.

Look at what happens to our line’s shape and length:

          /\
         /  \  <- Each triangle side is 1 unit
[---1---]    [---3---]

What we just did to a single line segment is the fundamental building block of the Koch Snowflake.

Instead of starting with just one flat line, imagine starting with a full, three-sided equilateral triangle. If you apply this exact same “triangle-adding” rule to all three sides simultaneously, and then repeat it infinitely on every new flat edge that appears, the shape blooms outward.

It forms a beautiful, infinitely complex geometric shape that looks exactly like a snowflake. This shape is our perfect model for understanding how a fractured coastline behaves.

If we count up our new 1-unit segments, we now have 4 units of total length instead of 3.

Mathematically, our line just grew by a factor of \(\frac{4}{3}\):

\[\text{Growth Factor} = \frac{\text{New Length}}{\text{Old Length}} = \frac{4}{3}\]

Step 2: The Sequence of the Perimeter

Because every single side increases by a factor of \(\frac{4}{3}\) at each step, we can calculate the perimeter at any step (\(n\)) using a geometric sequence:

  • Step 0 (Start): \(P_0 = 3s\)
  • Step 1: \(P_1 = 3s \cdot \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)\)
  • Step 2: \(P_2 = 3s \cdot \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^2\)
  • Step 3: \(P_3 = 3s \cdot \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^3\)

This gives us a general formula for the perimeter after \(n\) steps:

\[P_n = 3s \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^n\]

Every single time we repeat this trick on a flat segment, its length multiplies by \(\frac{4}{3}\). Because \(\frac{4}{3}\) is greater than 1, repeating this process forever causes the total perimeter (\(P\)) to explode to infinity:

\[\lim_{n \to \infty} P_n = \lim_{n \to \infty} 3s \left(\frac{4}{3}\right)^n = \infty\]

Step 3: Why the Snowflake Doesn’t Grow Infinitely Large

It feels like an infinite perimeter should mean an infinitely massive shape. But it doesn’t, thanks to a beautiful mathematical tug-of-war between length and area.

When you shrink a shape, its area shrinks much faster than its flat edge lengths. Let’s look at why:

1. The Triangles Shrink Fast

Think of area as a two-dimensional grid (length \(\times\) width). If you take a triangle and cut its side lengths down to \(\frac{1}{3}\) of their original size, you aren’t just losing length—you are losing space in both dimensions.

  • Length is cut by: \(\frac{1}{3}\)
  • Area is cut by: \(\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{3} = \left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{9}\)

Visually, this means it takes exactly 9 of our new tiny triangles packed together just to fill up the space of 1 single triangle from the previous step.

2. The Triangles Multiply Slowly

At each new step, every single straight line segment produces a new triangle. Since 1 line becomes 4 smaller segments, the number of new triangles we add is multiplied by 4 at each step.

The Tug-of-War: Putting It Together

Now, look at the balance of what is being added to the snowflake’s total area (\(\Delta A\)) at any step: We are adding 4 times as many triangles. But each individual triangle is 9 times smaller in area.

\[\Delta A = 4 \times \frac{1}{9} = \frac{4}{9}\]

Because \(\frac{4}{9}\) is less than 1, in fact, it’s less than half, the new area we add gets much smaller with every step.”.

Imagine painting the snowflake: in the first step, you might need a whole cup of paint. In the next step, you only need \(\frac{4}{9}\) of a cup. Then \(\frac{16}{81}\) of a cup. The amount of extra paint you need drops so fast toward zero that even if you paint forever, you will never finish a second cup.

To find the total area, we take the initial triangle area (\(A_0\)) and keep adding the area of every new layer of tiny triangles:

\[\text{Total Area} = A_0 + \frac{1}{3}A_0 + \left(\frac{1}{3}A_0 \times \frac{4}{9}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{3}A_0 \times \left(\frac{4}{9}\right)^2\right) + \dots\]

Factoring out the first layer’s area gives us a clean infinite geometric series:

\[\text{Total Area} = A_0 + \frac{1}{3}A_0 \left( 1 + \frac{4}{9} + \left(\frac{4}{9}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{4}{9}\right)^3 + \dots \right)\]

Using the sum formula for a shrinking infinite series, \(\frac{a}{1-r}\), where \(a = 1\) and \(r = \frac{4}{9}\), that entire endless chain inside the brackets perfectly collapses:

\[\text{Sum} = \frac{1}{1 - \frac{4}{9}} = \frac{9}{5}\]

Plugging this sum back into our main equation lets the fractions simplify beautifully:

\[\text{Total Area} = A_0 + \frac{1}{3}A_0 \left(\frac{9}{5}\right) = A_0 + \frac{3}{5}A_0 = \frac{8}{5}A_0\]

The Final Result

\[\text{Total Area} = \frac{8}{5} \times A_0 \]

The perimeter runs away to infinity, but the total area hits a solid brick wall.

Conclusion

Gabriel’s Horn and the Coastline Paradox blow our minds because they break all our everyday rules. In the real world, if you buy a gift box with a fixed size, you expect that a normal, finite amount of wrapping paper can cover it without forcing you into an infinite loop of existential dread.

Yet calculus exposes a wild truth: you can trap an infinite line inside a tiny, confined space. Infinity isn’t just an unreachable point at the end of a never-ending road,it can be neatly packaged and held right in the palm of your hand.

The Big Takeaways:

  • The Rate of the Shrink: Gabriel’s Horn works because of a high-speed race between dimensions. Volume collapses down to a hard limit (\(\pi\)) because its equations shrink at lightning speed, while the boundary expands forever.
  • The Reality of Nature: Smooth, perfect lines only exist in textbooks. The natural world is rugged, jagged, and broken. When standard calculus rules hit these rough edges, they shatter, forcing us to step into the mind-bending world of fractal geometry to measure reality.

Ultimately, these paradoxes prove that mathematical infinity isn’t a glitch in the matrix, it’s a feature. By finding the exact breaking points where traditional math stops working, we unlock the keys to mapping out entirely new dimensions.
