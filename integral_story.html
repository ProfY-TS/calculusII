The Story of Integrals: From Slices to Spheres

NoteGroup Information

Group: POP

Authors: Zhang Xinrong, Lin Yichen, Lai Yuhui, Wang Zitong

The Dream of Accumulation

Syracuse, ~250 BCE – A man and his circles

Long before the symbol \(\int\) was ever written, a man in Syracuse gazed at a circle and asked a deceptively simple question: What is its area?

Archimedes of Syracuse (287–212 BCE): “If I inscribe a polygon inside the circle and keep adding sides – 6, 12, 24, 48 – each polygon hugs the circle tighter. The area of the polygon approaches the area of the circle. I need only be patient enough to add infinitely many sides.”

This was the method of exhaustion – the first whisper of integration. Archimedes did not have limits, did not have algebra, did not have the number zero as we know it. What he had was geometry and patience. He approximated \(\pi\) by bounding the circle between inscribed and circumscribed 96-gons, proving that

\[3 \frac{10}{71} < \pi < 3 \frac{1}{7}\]

The method of exhaustion was brilliant but fragile – it required a new geometric construction for every new shape. What the world needed was a general method. A way to accumulate any quantity over any domain.

The Ghost of Departed Quantities

Nearly two millennia later, in the 1600s, mathematicians began chasing a strange idea: infinitesimals – quantities that were not zero, but smaller than any positive number. Cavalieri proposed that an area could be computed by summing infinitely many invisible “slices.” Kepler used such slices to calculate the volume of wine barrels.

Then came two men, working independently, who would change everything.

Isaac Newton (1642–1727): “I call it the method of fluxions. When a quantity flows – changes over time – its rate of change and its accumulated total are inverse operations. One undoes the other.”

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716): “I have devised a notation. The elongated S – \(\int\) – shall represent the summa, the sum of infinitely many infinitesimal parts: \(\int f(x) \, dx\). The \(dx\) reminds us we are summing over tiny intervals of \(x\).”

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus was born: differentiation and integration are inverse operations. This was the bridge between the rate of change and the total accumulation. The \(\int\) symbol – that elegant elongated S – became the emblem of an entire branch of mathematics.

\[\int_a^b f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a)\]

where \(F'(x) = f(x)\).

The Architecture of Sums

Germany, 1854 – Bernhard Riemann builds a foundation

For centuries, the integral was used but not truly defined. The infinitesimals that Leibniz loved were philosophically suspect – Berkeley called them “ghosts of departed quantities.” Mathematics needed rigor.

Enter Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866), who gave the integral a precise meaning without any reference to infinitesimals. His idea was beautiful in its simplicity:

Riemann: Divide \([a, b]\) into \(N\) subintervals. On each piece, sample the function at any point. Multiply the function value by the width of the piece. Sum them all up. Then let the mesh – the largest piece – shrink to zero.

A Student: And if the limit exists regardless of how I divide or where I sample?

Riemann: Then the function is integrable, and that limit is the integral.

\[\int_a^b f(x) \, dx := \lim_{\text{mesh} \to 0} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} f(x_j^*)(x_{j+1} - x_j)\]

This definition works. It is rigorous. And it reveals something profound: the integral is not about “infinitesimals” at all – it is about limits of finite sums. The area under a curve is the limit of better and better approximations. ^)

Riemann’s integral is a functional – a machine that eats a “reasonable” function \(f\) and produces a real number. This number represents the “size” or “cumulated quantity” of \(f\). The integral satisfies two crucial properties:

\[\int [\alpha f + \beta g] \, dx = \alpha \int f \, dx + \beta \int g \, dx \qquad \text{(Linearity)}\]

\[\text{if } f \le g \text{, then } \int f \, dx \le \int g \, dx \qquad \text{(Order)}\]

But here is the deeper insight: the Riemann integral is not limited to the real line. An integral can be viewed as

\[\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu\]

where \(\Omega\) is any domain and \(\mu\) is any measure – this is the general notion that unifies all integrals, from area under a curve to probability over a sample space.

The Second Dimension: Double Integrals

From curves to surfaces

Having conquered the real line, mathematicians looked around and asked: what if the domain is not an interval, but a region in the plane? What if we want to accumulate over a 2D area?

The answer is the double integral. The pattern is identical to Riemann’s: partition a rectangle into sub-rectangles, sample the function at points within each, multiply by the area of each sub-rectangle, sum, and take the limit.

\[\iint_D f(x, y) \, dA = \lim_{\text{mesh} \to 0} \sum_{i=0}^{M-1} \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} f(x_{i,j}^*) \, \Delta x_i \, \Delta y_j\]

Geometrically, this is the volume under the graph of \(f\) over \(D\) – a surface in 3D space. Physically, if \(f\) represents density, then the double integral gives the total mass of a flat plate.

Fubini’s Theorem: The Art of Iteration

But how do we actually compute a double integral? We cannot sum infinitely many rectangles one by one. Here, Guido Fubini (1879–1943) provides the key insight:

Fubini’s Theorem: “A double integral over a rectangle can be computed as an iterated integral – integrate with respect to one variable, then the other. The order does not matter.”

\[\iint_D f(x, y) \, dA = \int_c^d \left( \int_a^b f(x, y) \, dx \right) dy = \int_a^b \left( \int_c^d f(x, y) \, dy \right) dx\]

Fubini: Think of it like eating a rectangular cake. You can slice it into rows first, then cut each row into pieces. Or slice it into columns first, then cut each column. Either way, you eat the same cake.

For functions of product form \(f(x, y) = g(x)h(y)\), the double integral factorizes beautifully:

\[\iint g(x) h(y) \, dA = \int g(x) \, dx \cdot \int h(y) \, dy\]

Beyond Rectangles: General Domains

The real world is not made of rectangles. What if \(D\) is a disk? A region between two curves? The strategy is to approximate \(D\) with small rectangles, then let the approximation refine itself.

For practical computation, Fubini’s theorem extends to general domains by using variable bounds. If \(D\) is bounded between \(y = g_1(x)\) and \(y = g_2(x)\):

\[\iint_D f(x, y) \, dA = \int_{x=a}^b \left( \int_{y=g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x, y) \, dy \right) dx\]

The technique is called “shooting the ray” – imagine standing at the boundary of \(D\) and shooting a ray parallel to the \(y\)-axis. The ray enters \(D\) at \(y = g_1(x)\) and exits at \(y = g_2(x)\). These become your inner bounds.

Sometimes the order matters. Consider:

\[\int_0^2 \int_{y/2}^1 y e^{x^3} \, dx \, dy\]

The inner integral \(\int e^{x^3} \, dx\) has no elementary antiderivative! But:

\[\int_0^1 \int_0^{2x} y e^{x^3} \, dy \, dx = \frac{2}{3}(e - 1) \quad \checkmark\]

Symmetry is another powerful ally. If \(f\) is antisymmetric and \(D\) is symmetric in \(x\), then \(\iint_D f \, dA = 0\) – the positive and negative parts cancel exactly.

A Change of Perspective: Polar Coordinates

When the world is round, think in circles

Some problems are born circular. Disks, fans, annuli – regions that are rectangles in polar coordinates but tortured shapes in Cartesian coordinates. For these, a change of coordinates is not just convenient; it is essential.

The transformation is elegant:

\[x = r \cos \theta, \qquad y = r \sin \theta\]

\[\iint_D f(x, y) \, dA = \iint_{D'} f(r \cos \theta, r \sin \theta) \cdot \mathbf{r} \, dr \, d\theta\]

Where does the extra factor of \(r\) come from? A small “polar rectangle” at distance \(r\) from the origin has arc-length \(r \Delta\theta\) and radial width \(\Delta r\). Its area is approximately \(r \cdot \Delta r \cdot \Delta\theta\) – not just \(\Delta r \cdot \Delta\theta\). This \(r\) is the Jacobian of the polar transformation, and it is the reason the formula works.

The Gaussian Integral: A Masterpiece

To see the power of polar coordinates, consider one of the most famous integrals in all of mathematics. No antiderivative of \(e^{-x^2}\) exists in terms of elementary functions. Yet:

\[I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} \, dx = \text{?}\]

The trick is to square the integral, converting it to a double integral, then switch to polar coordinates:

\[I^2 = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)} \, dA\]

Switch to polar: \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\)

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2} r \, dr \, d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \pi\]

\[\therefore I = \sqrt{\pi}\]

One of the most important constants in probability and statistics – the normalizing factor of the Gaussian distribution – revealed by a simple change to polar coordinates. The integral of \(e^{-x^2}\) over all the real line is \(\sqrt{\pi}\). Not through clever antiderivatives, but through geometry.

Into the Third Dimension: Triple Integrals

From flatland to space

If a double integral accumulates over a 2D region, a triple integral accumulates over a 3D solid. The pattern is the same: partition a box into sub-boxes, sample, sum, and take the limit.

\[\iiint_R f(x, y, z) \, dV = \lim_{\text{mesh} \to 0} \sum f(x_{i,j,k}^*) \, \Delta x \, \Delta y \, \Delta z\]

Fubini’s theorem extends naturally – now with three iterated integrals:

\[\iiint_R f \, dV = \int_{z_1}^{z_2} \int_{y_1}^{y_2} \int_{x_1}^{x_2} f(x, y, z) \, dx \, dy \, dz\]

For general domains, the strategy remains: “shoot the ray” along the first integrating variable, then project onto the remaining dimensions.

Mass

\(m = \iiint_D \rho(x,y,z) \, dV\)

Density accumulated over volume gives total mass.

Volume

\(V = \iiint_D 1 \, dV\)

When density is 1, mass equals volume.

Center of Mass

\(\bar{x} = \frac{1}{m} \iiint x\rho \, dV\)

The average position, weighted by density.

Moment of Inertia

\(I_z = \iiint (x^2 + y^2) \, dm\)

Resistance to angular acceleration.

Each application is an integral – the same machinery, different physical interpretation. The integral is not just a mathematical tool; it is a bridge between mathematics and the physical world.

The Coordinate Alchemists

Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

Some solids are born cylinders. Some are born spheres. Forcing Cartesian coordinates upon them is like describing a sunset in binary – technically possible, but missing the point entirely.

Cylindrical Coordinates

\(x = r \cos \theta\) \(y = r \sin \theta\) \(z = z\)

\(dV = r \, dr \, d\theta \, dz\)

For cylinders, tubes, and anything with circular cross-sections.

Spherical Coordinates

\(x = \rho \sin\phi \cos\theta\) \(y = \rho \sin\phi \sin\theta\) \(z = \rho \cos\phi\)

\(dV = \rho^2 \sin\phi \, d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta\)

For spheres, cones, and anything centered at the origin.

The Jacobian: The Price of Transformation

Every coordinate transformation has a cost. When you change coordinates, areas and volumes stretch or compress. The factor that measures this distortion is called the Jacobian.

The Jacobian (personified): I am the determinant of the derivative matrix of the transformation. I tell you how much a tiny volume element is stretched when you change coordinates. Ignore me, and your integral will be wrong.

\[\iint_V f(x, y) \, dA = \iint_U f(\Phi(u, v)) \, |\det D\Phi(u, v)| \, du \, dv\]

Let us verify this for polar coordinates. The derivative matrix is:

\[D\Phi(r, \theta) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}\]

\[\det = r(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r \quad \checkmark\]

For spherical coordinates, the Jacobian is \(\rho^2 \sin\phi\). This arises because a small spherical “box” at distance \(\rho\) from the origin has sides of length \(d\rho\), \(\rho \, d\phi\), and \(\rho \sin\phi \, d\theta\). Their product gives the volume element.

The Jacobian is the unsung hero of multivariable calculus. It is the bridge that makes coordinate transformations correct, not merely convenient. Without it, the beautiful simplicity of polar or spherical integrals would collapse into nonsense.

An Application: The Rotating Sphere

As a final demonstration, consider a solid ball of uniform density and radius \(R\). Its moment of inertia about the \(z\)-axis – a measure of its resistance to spinning – is computed via a triple integral in spherical coordinates:

\[I_z = \iiint_D (x^2 + y^2) \, dV\]

In spherical: \(x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2 \sin^2\phi\)

\[= \int_0^{2\pi} \int_0^{\pi} \int_0^R \rho^2 \sin^2\phi \cdot \rho^2 \sin\phi \, d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta\]

\[= 2\pi \cdot \frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{R^5}{5} = \frac{2}{5} m R^2\]

This result – that a uniform sphere has \(I = \frac{2}{5} m R^2\) – is fundamental in physics, governing everything from the rotation of planets to the spin of a bowling ball. And it emerges naturally from the marriage of triple integrals and spherical coordinates.

The Grand Unification

Let us step back and see what we have built.

The integral began as a geometric trick – Archimedes exhausting a circle with polygons. It became a tool for calculating areas under curves, thanks to Newton and Leibniz. Riemann gave it a rigorous foundation. And then it grew.

1D – The Real Line

\(\int_a^b f(x) \, dx\) – area under a curve, mass of a rod, accumulation over time.

2D – The Plane

\(\iint_D f(x, y) \, dA\) – volume under a surface, mass of a plate, with Fubini’s theorem to compute iteratively.

Polar – When Circles Call

\(\iint f(r, \theta) \cdot r \, dr \, d\theta\) – the same volume, seen through circular eyes. The Gaussian integral yields \(\sqrt{\pi}\).

3D – Space

\(\iiint_D f(x, y, z) \, dV\) – mass of a solid, center of mass, moment of inertia.

Cylindrical & Spherical

\(dV = r \, dr \, d\theta \, dz\) or \(\rho^2 \sin\phi \, d\rho \, d\phi \, d\theta\) – coordinate transformations that make the intractable tractable.

Change of Variables

\(\int f(\Phi(u)) \, |\det D\Phi(u)| \, du\) – the Jacobian as the universal translator between coordinate systems.

The Moral of the Story: The integral is not a single formula. It is a philosophy: that the whole can be understood by summing its parts, no matter how the parts are shaped, no matter how many dimensions they inhabit. From the area of a circle to the inertia of a spinning planet, the integral speaks the same language.

\[\int_{\Omega} f \, d\mu = \text{the sum of all small things}\]

Every integral you compute – whether in Cartesian, polar, cylindrical, or spherical coordinates, whether over a line, a region, or a solid – is an instance of this single, elegant idea. The notation changes. The coordinates change. The dimension changes. But the heart of the integral remains the same:

Divide. Sample. Sum. Take the limit.

From Archimedes’ exhausted circles to Riemann’s rigorous sums, from Fubini’s iterated integrals to the Jacobian’s coordinate alchemy – this is the story of the integral. It is the story of humanity’s quest to measure the infinite, one small piece at a time.
