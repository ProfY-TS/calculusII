The Evolution of Integration: Why Was the Integral Invented?

NoteGroup Information

Group: Chicken
Authors: Ling Jia Shun, Jay Loh Yau Jie, Liu Zhihan

LING JIA SHUN
LIU ZHIHAN
JAY LOH YAU JIE

The Oldest Question: How Do You Measure a Curve?

Every civilisation that ever taxed a farmer’s land had to answer the same annoying question: how big is this field, exactly? If the field is a rectangle, multiply two sides and you are done before lunch. If it is a triangle, cut it in half and you are still done before lunch. But rivers do not flow in straight lines, and the boundary of a lake does not care about your ruler. The moment a shape acquires a curve, straightedge-and-triangle mathematics quietly gives up.

This is, in essence, the problem that eventually forced the integral into existence. Not because some genius woke up one morning and decided “today I shall invent the integral,” but because for over two thousand years, one generation of mathematicians after another kept running into the same wall: how do you assign an exact number to the area, length, or volume of something that refuses to be made of straight lines? The integral was not discovered in a single flash. It was assembled, argument by argument, objection by objection, across cultures and centuries. This is the story of that assembly line.

Archimedes and the Method of Exhaustion

Our story properly begins in the 3rd century BC, in the Greek city of Syracuse, with a mathematician who is more commonly remembered today for running naked through the streets shouting “Eureka.” Archimedes’ contribution to integration was rather more clothed and considerably more patient. 1

In his treatise Quadrature of the Parabola, Archimedes set out to find the exact area enclosed by a parabolic segment — a shape bounded by a parabola and a straight line. His method, later named the method of exhaustion (a name coined much later, in the 17th century, not by Archimedes himself), worked by relentlessly inscribing triangles inside the parabolic segment, each one filling in the gaps left by the last. 2

The genius of the method was not the infinite process itself — Archimedes was famously suspicious of “actual infinity” — but how he escaped it. He showed that the sum of these triangles approaches four-thirds of the area of the first, largest triangle, and then used a rigorous double contradiction argument to nail down the answer without ever saying the word “limit.” It is, quite literally, integration by exhaustion: you tire the shape into submission, one polygon at a time. 2

It is tempting to say Archimedes “invented calculus two thousand years early,” and in spirit he came remarkably close. But he lacked two crucial ingredients that would not arrive for another 1,900 years: a general algebraic notation, and a willingness to treat infinity as a working tool rather than something to be argued around.

A Mad Idea: Cavalieri’s Indivisibles

Fast-forward to 1635. An Italian mathematician named Bonaventura Cavalieri published a book with the wonderfully unmarketable title Geometria indivisibilibus continuorum nova quadam ratione promota — “Geometry, advanced in a new way by the indivisibles of the continuum.” Inside was an idea that should, by the standards of the time, have gotten him laughed out of the university: that any plane area could be thought of as being built out of infinitely many parallel line segments — “indivisibles” — stacked side by side like the pages of a very thin book. 3

This was scandalous. A line, by classical Greek standards, has zero width; stacking infinitely many zero-width objects should give you back precisely nothing, not an area. Cavalieri was not particularly bothered by this objection. His famous, almost defiant, response to critics of his rigor was:

“Rigour is the affair of philosophy, not of mathematics.” 4

In other words: it works, so let the philosophers argue about why.

And it did work, spectacularly. What we now call Cavalieri’s Principle — that two shapes with equal cross-sections at every height must have equal area or volume — let mathematicians compute areas and volumes that Archimedes’ exhaustion method would have taken a lifetime to reach one at a time. He just did not have the language of limits to justify why chopping a shape into infinitely thin slices should be allowed at all. That justification would take another two centuries to arrive — but the impatience to just start slicing, logic be damned, is exactly what pushed the story forward. 5

Newton, Leibniz, and a Very Public Feud

By the second half of the 17th century, the ingredients were all on the table: Archimedes’ exhaustion, Cavalieri’s indivisibles, decades of work by Fermat, Roberval, Pascal, Wallis and Barrow chipping away at tangents and areas from every angle imaginable. What was missing was the single, unifying insight that ties this whole essay together: that finding the area under a curve (integration) and finding the slope of a curve (differentiation) are, astonishingly, inverse operations of each other.

Two men arrived at this insight independently, within about a decade of each other. Isaac Newton developed his method of “fluxions” in England around 1665-1666, while sheltering from the plague at his family estate — probably the single most productive quarantine in the history of science. 6 Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed his own version in Germany and France in the 1670s, arriving at the same fundamental theorem through a completely different route, and — crucially — gave it the notation we still use today: the elongated “S” symbol \(\int\) (short for the Latin summa, “sum”) for the integral, and \(dx\) for an infinitesimal change. 7

Figure: Isaac Newton (left), painted by Godfrey Kneller (1689), and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (right). Both arrived at the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus independently — and then spent the rest of their lives arguing about who got there first.

What followed was one of the ugliest priority disputes in the history of science. British mathematicians, loyal to Newton, accused Leibniz of plagiarism; continental mathematicians returned fire. The argument dragged on for decades, well past both men’s deaths, and had a genuinely damaging side effect: British mathematics, clinging stubbornly to Newton’s clunkier fluxion notation out of national pride, fell noticeably behind continental Europe for the better part of a century, while French and German mathematicians happily built on Leibniz’s far more usable \(\int\) and \(dx\). 8

Whichever side of the Channel you rooted for, the outcome was the same: for the first time, integration was no longer a bag of clever one-off tricks for specific shapes. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus turned it into a general, mechanical method: to find the area under a curve, find its antiderivative and evaluate the difference at the two endpoints. Suddenly, problems that took Archimedes an entire treatise could be dispatched in a single line.

Berkeley’s Ghosts

Naturally, someone had to ruin the party, and that someone was an Irish bishop. In 1734, George Berkeley published a pamphlet with the delightfully petty subtitle A Discourse Addressed to an Infidel Mathematician, aimed squarely at the logical foundations of Newton’s fluxions and Leibniz’s infinitesimals. 9

Berkeley’s objection was simple and, honestly, fair: mathematicians were treating \(dx\) as a nonzero quantity when it was convenient (to divide by it) and as zero when it was convenient (to discard leftover terms). You cannot have it both ways. His mockery of these vanishing-yet-not-vanishing quantities produced one of the most quoted lines in the history of mathematics: he called them the “ghosts of departed quantities.” 10

The embarrassing part is that Berkeley was, mathematically, correct. A century’s worth of triumphant results — areas, volumes, planetary orbits, the tides — had all been computed using a foundation that, if you looked at it too closely, did not actually make logical sense. Calculus worked. Nobody could explain why it was allowed to work. Integration and continuity were both quietly resting on the same unexamined notion of “infinitely small,” and the 18th century simply chose to enjoy the results and postpone the audit.

Cauchy Draws the Line

The audit finally came in the early 19th century, and its auditor was Augustin-Louis Cauchy, professor at the École Polytechnique in Paris and arguably the single most influential textbook author calculus has ever had. 11

In his 1821 Cours d’Analyse and a follow-up 1823 lecture course, Cauchy did something nobody had properly done before: he defined the definite integral of a continuous function directly as a limit of sums, without appealing to any mysterious infinitesimal quantity. Chop the interval \([a, b]\) into \(n\) small pieces, form a sum of (function value) times (width of piece) for each piece, and then ask what happens to that sum as the pieces get finer and finer, and \(n \to \infty\). If that limit exists and is the same no matter how you chop, that limit is, by definition, the integral. [12]

This was the closest thing calculus had yet seen to Berkeley’s ghosts being properly exorcised: no more “infinitely small nudges,” only ordinary limits of ordinary finite sums, built entirely out of real numbers. Cauchy made the definite integral a precise object for continuous functions, but he still left a gap: continuity was a strong assumption, and many naturally occurring functions were too wild to fit inside it.

What about functions with a jump, a gap, a wild oscillation? That question would sit unanswered for another three decades. 12

Riemann’s Rectangles

The man who picked up that loose thread was Bernhard Riemann, in a rather unusual place to make a landmark contribution: his 1854 Habilitationsschrift (a qualifying dissertation for a German academic post), written primarily about a completely different subject — the representability of functions by trigonometric (Fourier) series. 13

To even ask which functions can be represented as a Fourier series, Riemann first needed to know precisely which functions could be integrated at all — including functions far stranger than the well-behaved, continuous ones Cauchy had handled. His solution, now taught in every second-year calculus course under his name, generalises Cauchy’s construction: partition \([a, b]\) into subintervals of possibly unequal width, pick any point you like inside each subinterval, and form the sum

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*) \, \Delta x_i . \]

If this sum converges to the same value regardless of how the partition is chosen or refined, that common value is the Riemann integral of \(f\). 14

This single change — allowing the sample point in each subinterval to be arbitrary, and demanding convergence regardless of partition — massively widened the class of “integrable” functions beyond continuous ones. It is, quite literally, the picture every calculus student draws first: rectangles, of width \(\Delta x\) and height \(f(x_i^*)\), stacked side by side under a curve, getting thinner and more numerous until their combined area locks onto a single number.

Riemann’s definition made integration work for functions that are discontinuous in many places, but it still failed for functions whose bad behavior was too pervasive. The Dirichlet function, which is 1 on rationals and 0 on irrationals, is the classic example: it is discontinuous everywhere, and Riemann’s rectangles simply have no stable height assignment for it.

Lebesgue and the Unfinished Story

Riemann’s definition was a huge advance, but it was not the end of the story — because some functions are simply too badly behaved even for it. The most notorious troublemaker is the Dirichlet function, which outputs 1 at every rational number and 0 at every irrational number. It is discontinuous at literally every single point of its domain, and no matter how you chop its interval into pieces, each piece contains both rationals and irrationals — so the height you “should” use for each rectangle is hopelessly ambiguous. Riemann’s rectangles simply cannot get a grip on it.

The fix came from Henri Lebesgue, who in 1901-1902 flipped the entire strategy on its head. Instead of chopping up the \(x\)-axis (the domain) into little intervals, as Riemann did, Lebesgue proposed chopping up the \(y\)-axis (the range) into little intervals, and asking how large — in a new, carefully defined sense called measure — the set of inputs is that produces each output range. 15

Every Riemann-integrable function is also Lebesgue-integrable, and the two definitions agree whenever both apply — so nothing from the earlier chapters of this story was thrown away. Lebesgue’s integral did not replace Riemann’s; it absorbed it, and then kept going further than Riemann ever could.

Lebesgue’s insight was the key shift: integrate by measuring the size of the set of inputs that give each output value, rather than by subdividing the domain into small \(x\)-intervals. That allowed him to treat functions that are discontinuous almost everywhere, as long as the set of places where they behave badly is small in the sense of measure. 15

So, Why Was the Integral Invented?

If you have made it this far, you may notice that no single person actually “invented” the integral. Archimedes exhausted a parabola without a concept of limits. Cavalieri sliced shapes into indivisibles he could not fully justify. Newton and Leibniz turned area and slope into two sides of the same coin, then spent the rest of their lives fighting over who got there first. Berkeley called their infinitesimals ghosts, and he was right to. Cauchy exorcised the ghosts for continuous functions with a rigorous limit. Riemann extended that rigor to a much wider zoo of functions. And Lebesgue, faced with functions too wild even for Riemann, rebuilt the whole idea from a different angle entirely.

The integral, in other words, was not invented. It was renegotiated, again and again, across more than two thousand years, every time someone found a shape, a function, or an objection that the previous definition could not survive. That, perhaps, is the real reason the integral exists at all: not because one genius needed to measure a curve, but because mathematics, as a community stretched across centuries and continents, refused to accept “it works, don’t ask why” as a final answer for very long.
